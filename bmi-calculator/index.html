About BMI

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a simple way to estimate whether your weight is in a healthy range for your height. It's widely used by doctors and health organizations as a quick screening tool — not a diagnosis, but a useful starting point.

How BMI is calculated

The formula is straightforward: divide your weight in kilograms by your height in meters squared. For imperial measurements, the same formula is used with a conversion factor (weight in lbs ÷ height in inches² × 703).

Limitations of BMI

BMI is a useful screening tool but has real limitations. It doesn't account for muscle mass, bone density, age, or where body fat is distributed. Some common examples where BMI can be misleading:

  • Athletes and bodybuilders often have a high BMI due to muscle, not fat.
  • Older adults may have a "normal" BMI but carry more body fat than younger people at the same BMI.
  • Different ethnicities have different health risk thresholds — some Asian health organizations use lower cutoffs.

If your BMI falls outside the normal range, talk to a doctor before drawing any conclusions — especially if you're very active or have a muscular build.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher BMI always bad?

Not necessarily. Athletes often have a high BMI due to muscle mass. BMI is a screening tool, not a definitive health measure. A doctor will consider many other factors alongside BMI.

What's a healthy BMI for women vs. men?

The standard BMI ranges (18.5–24.9 for normal weight) apply to both men and women. However, women naturally carry more body fat than men at the same BMI, so some clinicians use body fat percentage alongside BMI for a fuller picture.

How often should I check my BMI?

Since BMI depends on height (which doesn't change for adults), tracking changes monthly is usually sufficient. Daily fluctuations in weight are mostly water and don't reflect real changes in body composition.
